# What to build next in games — the research, and what it implies

Twelve games ship today. This is an argument about the next eight, grounded in
what actually happens to Indian retail traders rather than in what is pleasant
to build.

---

## 1. The finding that should reorganise the roadmap

SEBI's own studies of the equity F&O segment:

| | |
|---|---|
| Individual traders who lost money, FY22–FY24 | **93%** |
| Same, FY25 | **91%** |
| Aggregate net losses, FY25 | **₹1,05,603 crore** (up 41% on FY24) |
| Traders earning >₹1 lakh profit after costs, FY22–24 | **1 in 100** |
| Top 3.5% of loss-makers | **₹28 lakh** average loss each |
| **Loss-makers who kept trading after consecutive losing years** | **more than 75%** |
| Traders under 30, FY24 | **43%** (up from 31% in FY23) |
| Traders from beyond the top 30 cities | **72%** |
| Traders declaring income under ₹5 lakh | **75%+** |
| Transaction costs paid, FY22–24 | **₹50,000 crore** (avg ₹26,000/trader in FY24) |

Read the bolded row again. The decisive failure is not that people cannot read a
candle. It is that **they do not stop.** Nine in ten lose, and three in four of
those come back next year and do it again.

Our twelve games almost all teach *how to trade well*. Chart Replay, Order
Gauntlet, Candle Sprint, Payoff Builder — every one assumes the question "should
I be doing this at all?" has been settled in the affirmative. Only Risk Roulette
touches ruin, and it does so through position size rather than through
persistence.

**So the next tranche should train the quit decision, self-assessment, and
resistance to being sold to** — the three things that separate the 7% from the
93% — and only then add more instrument mechanics.

## 2. What the evidence says about whether games can teach this at all

Two results matter, because they say the ambitious games are worth building.

**Cognitive bias is trainable, durably, from one sitting.** Morewedge et al.
(2015) ran longitudinal experiments where a single serious game produced medium
to large reductions in confirmation bias, bias blind spot and correspondence
bias — measured immediately, and still present two to three months later.
Follow-up work (Sellier, Scopelliti, Morewedge) showed the effect transferring
to real managerial decisions, not merely to the test. This is the strongest
evidence in the field, and it is evidence for the game we have invested in
*least*: Bias Buster appears in only 2 of 89 lessons.

**Calibration is trainable and measurable.** In the IARPA tournament behind the
Good Judgment Project, brief probabilistic-reasoning training plus immediate
accuracy feedback measurably improved forecasts, and superforecasters ran Brier
scores 20–30% below the median forecaster. Calibration is scoreable, improves
with feedback, and is exactly the skill a trader needs and never measures.

**What makes an educational game work**, from the wider literature: immediate
feedback, difficulty held in the zone of proximal development, and *desirable
difficulty* — spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice. Friction is the point,
not a flaw. A game that feels frictionless is usually teaching nothing.

## 3. Coverage today

Games embedded across the 89 lessons:

```
candle-sprint     7      cost-cutter       2
circuit-breaker   6      bias-buster       2
the-long-game     5      payoff-builder    1
risk-roulette     5      margin-call       0
order-gauntlet    5      expiry-day        0
earnings-roulette 5
chart-replay      5
```

Two problems, both fixable without writing a new game:

- **`margin-call` and `expiry-day` are orphans.** They exist at `/play` and no
  lesson reaches them. Given that 43% of F&O traders are under 30 and leverage
  is how the ₹28 lakh losses happen, margin-call being unreachable from the
  curriculum is the cheapest defect on this page to fix.
- **The distribution is upside down.** Candle Sprint leads at 7. It trains the
  most superficial skill we teach — speed of pattern naming — while Bias Buster,
  the one mechanic with published durable-transfer evidence behind it, sits at 2.

## 4. The eight games, in priority order

Priority = (loss actually prevented) × (strength of evidence) ÷ (build cost).
Reuse matters: the replay, fill, cost and process-scoring engines already exist,
so several of these are content and rules rather than new machinery.

---

### P1 · Edge or Luck — can you tell a real record from a coin flip?

**Trains:** the most valuable statistical instinct a trader can have.

You are shown a track record — equity curve, win rate, 40 trades, a
confident-sounding strategy note — and must decide: edge, or noise? Commit, then
the reveal. Several of the records were generated by literal coin flips with a
plausible-looking curve. Others are real edges hidden under an ugly drawdown.

Then it turns the mirror: it runs the same test on **the learner's own filed
runs** and reports how many more trades they would need before their win rate
could be distinguished from chance. For nearly everyone the answer is "far more
than you have."

**Why it is first:** this is the mechanism behind "more than 75% kept trading
after consecutive losing years." People cannot see that their sample proves
nothing, so a losing year reads as bad luck rather than as evidence. Nothing
else on this list attacks the central failure so directly.

**Reuses:** `game_runs` history, process scorer. Mostly statistics and copy.

---

### P2 · The Tout — spot the pump before it dumps

**Trains:** resistance to being sold to. The most protective game here.

A feed of Telegram messages, YouTube thumbnails, X posts, WhatsApp forwards.
Some are ordinary. Some are a pump-and-dump in progress. Call them, and give
reasons — "guaranteed", "limited seats", lifestyle shots, unverifiable P&L
screenshots, a small-cap with no liquidity, a target with no stop, a SEBI
registration number that does not resolve. Then the chart plays forward and you
see who was holding at the top.

Grounded in a real SEBI interim order (May 2026) against a network that ran 82
stocks through handles with 54,000+ followers. **62% of Indian retail investors
follow finfluencers; roughly 2% of those are registered.** Our audience is 43%
under 30 and 72% from outside the big metros — precisely the people that order
describes as the exit liquidity.

**Why it scores so high:** it is the most *fun* game on the list — it is a
whodunnit — and simultaneously the one most likely to prevent a real loss.

**Reuses:** replay engine for the aftermath chart. Content-heavy: the messages
must be written, and must be uncomfortably realistic.

---

### P3 · How Sure Are You? — calibration, scored

**Trains:** the difference between a view and a certainty.

Every question is a probability, not a direction. "NIFTY closes above 24,800 on
Friday — how likely?" Answer 0–100%. Answer fifty over a season and get a
**Brier score**, a calibration curve (when you said 80%, you were right 55% of
the time), and a discrimination score. The reveal is nearly always the same and
nearly always lands: you are systematically overconfident in the 70–90% band.

This is the most direct expression of the site's own rule that process outranks
profit, and the natural companion metric to the leaderboard.

**Why:** the only game here with a published effect size behind its exact
mechanic. It is also cheap — no simulation, just questions, resolution and
scoring.

**Reuses:** market data for resolution, leaderboard for ranking on Brier score.

---

### P4 · Sit Out — a season where the winning move is usually no move

**Trains:** the quit decision, and the cost of needing action.

Sixty trading days over a real historical stretch, a finite account, real costs.
Setups are graded, and most days offer nothing worth taking. Trading a C-grade
setup is *allowed* — it simply loses money quietly to costs and variance. The
scoring rewards trades **not** taken. A boredom meter rises on flat days, and the
game reports at the end how many of your trades arrived while it was high.

It ends with the number that matters: what you made, versus what you would have
made taking only the A setups, versus having done nothing at all. For most
players, nothing wins.

**Why:** ₹50,000 crore of transaction costs over three years, ₹26,000 per trader
per year, on accounts belonging to people declaring under ₹5 lakh of income.
Overtrading is not a nuance here, it is the bill.

**Reuses:** replay engine, cost engine, fill engine — nearly all of it exists.

---

### P5 · Tilt — the engineered losing streak

**Trains:** behaviour inside a drawdown, which is where accounts actually die.

The deck is stacked: you lose the first five trades regardless of how well you
decided. What is measured is not P&L but **deviation** — did position size grow
after the losses? Did stops widen? Did the gap between trades shrink? Did you
abandon the plan you wrote at the start?

The score is a discipline score, so it is possible to lose money and still win
outright.

PLAN.md §P5 already names "tilt scenarios" as the honest answer to the fact that
paper trading cannot manufacture fear. This is that game.

**Reuses:** replay engine with a biased sampler, process scorer.

---

### P6 · The Order Book — be the market maker for ninety seconds

**Trains:** where price comes from, and why your fill was worse than the screen.

You quote a two-sided market into a live book. Orders arrive. Quote too wide and
nobody trades with you; too tight and you are run over by someone who knows
something. Inventory accumulates and must be managed. Then the sides flip: you
are the retail trader hitting the book, feeling the spread and the impact from
the other end.

**Why:** "Why does the price move?" is Stage 1 of the syllabus and is currently
taught with prose and a widget. It is the most mechanistic idea in the
curriculum, the one most improved by being played, and it explains slippage
permanently.

**Reuses:** the fill engine's spread and impact model. Needs a new book
simulator — the most expensive build here, and worth it.

---

### P7 · Red Flags — find the fraud in the filings

**Trains:** fundamental analysis as a defensive skill.

Two companies side by side, real-shaped financials. Find the tells: receivables
growing faster than revenue, profit with no operating cash flow, promoter pledge
rising, an auditor resigning, related-party transactions, a sudden change of
accounting year. Scored on flags caught and flags invented.

**Why:** the fundamentals track is text-heavy and has no game at all. It is also
the only game here defending against the *slow* way retail loses money — holding
something that was never real — rather than the fast F&O way.

**Reuses:** little. Content-heavy but static; no simulation needed.

---

### P8 · The Tax Year — keep what you made

**Trains:** the last mile nobody teaches, and it is entirely India-specific.

A year of closed positions and one month left. Harvest losses or not. Which lots
to sell. STCG at 20% versus LTCG at 12.5% above the ₹1.25 lakh exemption. What
changes if you sell on 30 March rather than 2 April. Speculative versus
non-speculative business income if you traded F&O, and the audit threshold that
follows.

**Why:** the cost engine already models STT, stamp duty, exchange, SEBI, GST and
DP charges to the rupee. Tax is the one remaining leak, it is entirely
learnable, and every rupee saved is certain rather than probabilistic — the only
guaranteed return on this site.

**Reuses:** cost engine, `game_fills` history.

---

## 5. Two things to fix before writing any of them

1. **Embed `margin-call` and `expiry-day` in lessons.** They are built, tested,
   deployed and unreachable from the curriculum.
2. **Rebalance towards Bias Buster.** It has the best evidence of any mechanic we
   own and the least usage. Candle Sprint at 7 embeds against Bias Buster at 2 is
   exactly backwards.

Both are hours, not weeks, and they raise the value of the existing twelve more
than a thirteenth game would.

## 6. What I would not build

- **A multiplayer live-trading tournament.** It rewards variance and teaches the
  exact lesson PLAN.md §7 rule 4 exists to prevent: the person who bet the
  account and got lucky wins the week.
- **Streaks and badges on P&L.** Same objection. Streaks on *practice* are fine;
  streaks on *profit* are a slot machine.
- **More pattern-recognition games.** Candle Sprint already reports honest hit
  rates, and the honest answer is that the edge is thin. A second game saying so
  more loudly is not more learning.
